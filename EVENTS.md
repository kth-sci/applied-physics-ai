### Session design

**Session 1 — Tools and foundations (1 h).** Jonas and Wei. Thirty minutes on
current state-of-the-art tools, GitHub for people who do not use it daily,
running local language models on the department's DGX Spark, and how to write an
agent skill. Thirty minutes of open discussion. Paired with a printed cheat sheet
— GitHub basics, how to get AI access at APHYS, the anatomy of a skill, and a
short worked tutorial — sent out as a pre-read so the hour is not spent on setup.

**Session 2 — Experience sharing (1.5 h).** What people have actually used AI for
over the past year, including what did not work. Pitfalls are at least as useful
as successes, and both are explicitly invited. Pre-work: every participant brings
one concrete case to share.

**Session 3 — Agentic mini hackathon (4 h).** The centrepiece, run in groups that
deliberately mix newcomers with experienced users so that experience is
demonstrated rather than lectured. Candidate topics:

1. Research-related agent
2. Grading-assistant agent
3. Teaching-assistant agent
4. Email-crunching agent
5. Processing sensitive information using a local language model on the DGX Spark

Five topics across 30 participants gives groups of roughly six. Each group
presents a brief demo in session 3c on day 2. Every group commits at least one
`SKILLS.md` to the shared repository — a requirement rather than an aspiration,
and what makes the retreat's output outlast the two days. A budget of $3,000 in
API tokens is provided, allocated per group, and the DGX Spark is brought on site
for the local-model track.

**Keynote — industrial perspective (1 h + dinner discussion).** An external
speaker from industry on how hiring and the working environment have changed, and
what the department should prioritise to make its students competitive. The
discussion continues over dinner. Speaker to be announced once confirmed.

**Session 4 — Ethics and departmental guidelines (2 h).** Case-study discussion in
groups, covering responsible use of AI in teaching and learning; how far AI should
go in lab reports, thesis writing, data analysis and communication; who owns data
shared with an AI system; whether an AI-generated idea can be patented; and the
human-AI working relationship, including the role of humans in an AI-dominated
industry. Cases are prepared in advance and reviewed by the organisers before use.

Closing with this session is deliberate: it produces the departmental guidelines,
and it benefits from a day and a half of shared experience preceding it. A
skeleton guidelines document will be drafted in advance so the session edits a
real draft rather than starting from a blank page.
